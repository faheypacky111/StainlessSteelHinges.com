Stainless steel vs brass hinges

Updated November 2026 · independent guidance

Stainless steel lasts longer and is stronger, so it's the better choice for exterior doors, gates, heavy or busy doors, and anywhere near salt water. Solid brass wins on looks and is a good, rust-free choice for decorative interior doors. If you're unsure, choose stainless.

Which lasts longer?

Stainless steel lasts longer in most real-world use. It's harder than brass, so the knuckles and pin wear more slowly on heavy or busy doors, and it shrugs off weather. Indoors, solid brass can also last for decades, especially on lighter doors that don't see much traffic. Outdoors and near salt water, 316 stainless is the safer long-term choice. When to choose stainless and when to choose brass

Choose stainless steel if…

  • The door is exterior, or the hinge is on a gate or shed.
  • You live within about 5 miles of salt water or have a pool (use 316).
  • The door is heavy, solid-core, or used all day. Pair it with ball bearings.
  • You want a modern look and almost no upkeep.

Choose solid brass if…

  • The door is interior and appearance comes first.
  • You're matching other brass hardware or a traditional style.
  • The door is light to medium weight with normal traffic.
  • You like a warm patina or don't mind polishing.

Either way, use screws of the same metal as the hinge. Mixing metals outdoors can cause galvanic corrosion. For stainless, see which screws to use.

Solid brass vs brass-plated: check before you buy

Many cheap "brass" hinges are steel with a thin brass-colored coating. Once that coating wears or chips, the steel underneath can rust, which defeats the point of choosing brass. Check the listing for "solid brass," or test it with a magnet: solid brass isn't magnetic. The same trap exists with zinc-plated hinges. If you want a gold look without the upkeep, some stainless hinges come in a brass-colored finish, though the coating can still scratch over time.

Frequently asked questions

What type of hinge won't rust?

Solid brass and stainless steel hinges don't rust in normal use. Brass contains no iron, and stainless protects itself with a thin chromium-oxide layer. For outdoor and coastal doors, 316 stainless is the most rust-resistant common choice. Avoid zinc-plated, brass-plated and painted steel hinges outdoors; once the coating wears, the steel underneath rusts. See our rust-resistant hinge picks.

What is the strongest hinge?

For doors, a heavy-weight stainless steel ball-bearing butt hinge is the strongest common choice. Its thicker leaves and bearings carry heavy doors without sagging. For gates and metal frames, weld-on barrel hinges are stronger still. Size, leaf thickness and the number of hinges matter more than the metal alone. See our heavy-duty hinge guide.

Do brass hinges rust?

Solid brass contains no iron, so it doesn't rust. It tarnishes to a darker patina instead. Brass-plated steel hinges are different: the plating wears through and the steel underneath can rust.

Are brass hinges stronger than stainless?

No. Stainless steel is harder and stronger, which is why it's preferred for heavy and high-traffic doors. Brass is softer, so its knuckles wear faster under heavy use. It's best on lighter, decorative doors.

Can you use brass hinges outside?

Solid brass works outdoors in many climates and will weather to a darker patina. Near salt water, 316 stainless holds up better. Never use brass-plated steel hinges outside; the plating fails and the steel rusts.

How can I tell solid brass from brass-plated?

Use a magnet. Solid brass isn't magnetic, so a magnet won't stick. If it sticks firmly, the hinge is steel with a brass-colored plating. (Don't use the same test on stainless; some stainless hinges are slightly magnetic. Here's why.)
